I re-reviewed the updated branch against both the earlier version I analyzed and the current vocabulary-assessment literature. The change is substantial: this is no longer mainly a frequency-informed Rasch prototype. It has become an **uncertainty-aware adaptive assessment system with a much clearer measurement contract, local longitudinal personalization, empirical-calibration infrastructure, and an explicit validation/release framework**.

There is also one important procedural point up front: PR #9’s current body is now materially stale. It still describes the earlier model in which direct answers were effectively authoritative and the interaction was based around revealing before final scoring.

The branch documentation itself now gives an unusually careful account of what the model means and does not mean: answers are probabilistic evidence, item difficulty has uncertainty, coverage is posterior-predictive, domain effects remain experimental, warm starts are local, and synthetic tests are not treated as human validation.

### The response protocol is much better

My strongest criticism of the previous implementation was hindsight contamination. That has been fixed quite well.

The reader now makes the initial judgment **before seeing the definition or book context**. "I know it" does not immediately become a positive observation: the definition is revealed and the reader then confirms whether their intended meaning was actually correct. Unknown/unsure responses are recorded before learning content appears. There is also an optional typed-meaning path.

That was the right change, but the scientific picture is worth qualifying. Stewart et al. found meaning recall and meaning recognition psychometrically distinguishable and found recall added predictive value for reading beyond recognition. ([Sage Journals][1]) A new 2026 study by Robles-García et al., however, found the opposite in its population: meaning recognition predicted reading better, particularly once lower-frequency 4K–5K vocabulary was included. ([ScienceDirect][2])

So I **would not make typed recall mandatory**. Your current architecture—low-friction self-verification in production plus the ability to obtain typed meaning-recall criterion data in a validation study—is actually a good response to an empirical literature that is not completely settled.

### Direct answers are no longer treated as infallible truth

This is another major improvement.

The current model differentiates evidence such as verified-known, typed-verified-known, verified-unknown/partial, reported-unknown, unsure, and migrated legacy answers. Those observations have different assumed reliabilities rather than forcing the corresponding item to exactly 0 or 1.

Answered-item probabilities are reconstructed using the observation evidence and a leave-one-out-style posterior rather than being hard-coded.

This directly attacks the response-noise failure I identified previously.

### Item difficulty uncertainty is now modeled explicitly

Previously, a frequency rank was transformed into one exact difficulty number. That implied far more certainty than the frequency corpus justified.

Now an item has a `VocabularyItemDifficultyPrior` with a mean **and standard deviation**. Ranked words receive rank-dependent uncertainty; unranked words receive a hard mean but a much wider `SD=1.5`. Empirical calibration can later override the frequency prior.

The assessment integrates over this uncertainty with Gaussian quadrature rather than pretending the estimated difficulty is exact.

This is particularly well supported by current CAT research. A 2025 *Behavior Research Methods* paper specifically examined item-calibration error in adaptive testing and found that treating estimated item parameters as known can underestimate uncertainty and reduce ability-estimation accuracy, especially when calibration error is substantial. ([PubMed][3])

It also agrees with vocabulary-specific work. Ha, Nguyen and Stoeckel found that frequency was **not** the strongest predictor of word difficulty in their learner population. ([Sage Journals][4]) Canning, McLean and Vitta found frequency, cognate status and prevalence all contributed significantly, with L1-dependent relative complexity being relevant to generalizability. ([PRESSto][5])

So the new interpretation is much healthier:

[
\text{frequency} \rightarrow \text{prior information about difficulty}
]

rather than:

[
\text{frequency rank} = \text{true difficulty}.
]

### The coverage calculation is now considerably more coherent

The previous version calculated a conservative coverage quantity using the lower tail of θ plus a weighted normal approximation.

The new version samples from the full posterior predictive process. It includes uncertainty in θ, item difficulty, soft evidence, and latent item knowledge, and obtains the conservative coverage measure from the fifth percentile of 512 deterministic posterior-predictive samples.

That is much closer to the quantity the interface claims to expose.

For small candidate sets it can compare against exact subset enumeration; larger inventories use a worst-tail greedy construction followed by redundant-card deletion. The documentation correctly stops calling this globally minimal for large inventories.

### Lemma + POS was the right lexical-unit change

The system now defines stable lexical identity from language, lemma and part of speech, while reserving a sense key for later use. Production sense splitting is deliberately absent.

The document index now obtains both lemma and lexical class. It conservatively assigns POS only when the leading tag hypothesis is sufficiently dominant; uncertain cases remain `unknown`.

This aligns closely with the recent word-family literature. A 2024 *System* study explicitly defines a lemma as a baseword in a particular POS plus its same-POS inflections and found weak correspondence between baseword and derivational-family knowledge even in meaningful contexts. ([ScienceDirect][6]) John Read's 2023 assessment review likewise notes increasing evidence against automatically crediting entire word families and highlights lemmas as a more defensible basic lexical unit. ([Sage Journals][7])

So I would **keep lemma+POS**.

I would also keep same-POS sense splitting disabled for now.

### Cross-book personalization is now real

The branch maintains a separate, local per-language reader profile. It requires sufficient completed sessions, verified evidence and freshness before becoming eligible. The stored posterior is smoothed, mixed with a generic prior, and new books still contain direct tail validations.

The UI clearly allows the reader to inspect and reset these profiles.

This is a substantial product improvement because your current synthetic results reveal just how expensive a cold assessment is: many cold scenarios still end up close to the 80-question ceiling, whereas the warm profile reduces question count dramatically.

### The domain work is more conservative than the screenshots implied—and that's good

You now have pinned fiction, news and Wikipedia/reference resources and a domain detector. But production difficulty **does not yet use the domain blend**. The experimental detector/picker records domain metadata, while the ordinary calibrated/general provider still supplies actual difficulty.

I think this is the correct implementation.

A broad contemporary review of contextual diversity found highly consistent effects for word-form processing, but much more mixed effects for word-meaning processing, with results depending on task and the exact measure of diversity. ([Springer Link][8]) That is precisely the sort of literature that argues against inventing a `30% fiction + 70% general` psychometric formula and treating it as established fact.

The domain resources should first become **features whose incremental predictive value is empirically tested**, not production priors by assumption.

### The research/validation infrastructure is now one of the strongest parts

The ordinary research export is deliberately privacy-minimized. It can contain pseudonymous participant ID, optional L1/proficiency, lexical identity, domain, difficulty information, evidence/protocol version and session ordinal, but omits document title, path, context, definitions, typed meanings, exact timestamps and account identity.

Calibration packs are also inert by default: fitting produces `reviewed: false`; reviewed production items need minimum learner support, bounded standard error and no flagged material DIF.

Most importantly, the proposed real-learner validation study correctly does **not** use the production self-verification labels as its own ground truth. It calls for pre-reveal typed meanings/translations, frozen scoring rubrics, two blinded human raters, participant-and-document holdouts, complete small-document audits, stratified large-document audits and delayed retesting.

This is very much in line with the vocabulary-assessment community's repeated warning that intended interpretations need to be explicitly validated for the target population and purpose. ([Cambridge University Press][9])

# 2. Where I would improve the current implementation

The core architecture is now strong enough that I would **stop adding speculative features** and concentrate on measurement consistency, calibration and release robustness.

## Priority 0: separate three different kinds of uncertainty

Right now `errorFloor` does more than one conceptual job.

You really have at least three different mechanisms:

[
P(K_j \mid \theta,b_j)
]

**latent knowledge model**, including idiosyncratic exceptions;

[
P(R \mid K_j, evidenceType)
]

**measurement/response reliability**;

and

[
P(b_j \mid features)
]

**item-difficulty uncertainty**.

The current implementation does a good job separating the third one, but the first two remain partly entangled: validation contradictions modify an `errorFloor` that both flattens latent knowledge predictions and caps evidence reliability.

I'd split it into something approximately like:

[
\epsilon_{\text{knowledge}}
]

for person-item exceptions to the Rasch curve, and

[
\rho_{\text{verifiedKnown}},
\rho_{\text{reportedUnknown}},
\rho_{\text{unsure}},\ldots
]

for observation reliability.

Then estimate them from different evidence:

* tail-validation mismatches mostly inform model/idiosyncrasy error;
* independently scored criterion responses inform evidence reliability;
* item-level calibration data informs difficulty.

This will make the model both more interpretable and easier to validate.

## Priority 0: make the offline fitter use the same observation model as production

There is a concrete inconsistency here.

Production now treats answer categories as uncertain observations.

The offline calibration script currently maps:

```text
verifiedKnown → 1
typedVerifiedKnown → 1
verifiedUnknownOrPartial → 0
reportedUnknown → 0
unsure → 0.25
```

and fits those values directly.

That means empirical calibration would be trained under a **different measurement model than the one used at runtime**.

I would change the calibration likelihood to:

[
P(E_{ij}\mid\theta_i,b_j,\rho_E)
]

where `E` is the observed evidence category.

Ideally, the reliability parameters themselves are estimated from the independently scored pilot.

This becomes especially important because the evidence protocol is one of your biggest sources of misspecification.

## Priority 0: strengthen the calibration-data collection design

Your normal CAT asks the most useful words for the current reader. That is excellent for the reader, but not automatically excellent for estimating population item parameters.

Different words will be exposed to very different ability ranges. Tail-validation questions are also intentionally non-random. Your current research export doesn't preserve enough of the *selection decision* to fully reconstruct that process.

CAT calibration literature explicitly treats **who receives which calibration item** as a separate design problem; random seeding, adaptive item-centered designs and compound optimal designs are used precisely because efficient ability measurement and efficient item calibration are not the same objective. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][10])

I would reserve perhaps **5–10% of questions** as calibration/exploration slots once research mode is enabled.

For those records, export privacy-safe fields such as:

```text
questionOrdinal
selectionType:
    calibration
    tailValidation
    adaptiveLoss
    initialCalibration

predictedKnownBeforeAnswer
thetaBinBeforeAnswer
```

No document text or identity is required.

Eventually you can use item-centered calibration: if *abrogate* already has plenty of high-θ responses but almost none from moderate-θ readers, preferentially seed it when such a reader encounters it in a book.

That will produce a dramatically healthier empirical item bank.

## Priority 0: replace the current DIF heuristic before making production fairness claims

The current fitter marks DIF by comparing mean residuals across L1/proficiency groups with a fixed difference threshold after a minimum group count.

That's a useful smoke test, but it isn't strong enough to justify "no material DIF" as a psychometric claim.

The 2025 systematic review of 83 L2-assessment DIF studies specifically warns about weak DIF methodology and speculative explanations. It identifies Rasch procedures, logistic regression, Mantel–Haenszel, IRT and SIBTEST as established approaches while arguing for better reporting and consideration of heterogeneous language backgrounds. ([Sage Journals][11])

I'd implement, offline:

* logistic-regression DIF and/or Rasch likelihood-ratio analysis;
* explicit uniform versus non-uniform DIF;
* effect sizes, not only significance;
* multiple-comparison control;
* iterative anchor purification;
* minimum group sizes;
* separate L1 and proficiency analyses rather than only concatenated `L1|proficiency` strings.

And don't necessarily discard every item displaying L1 DIF.

For something like an English/French cognate, DIF may reflect a *real difference in learning burden*. Canning et al.'s results are directly relevant here. ([ERIC][12])

Eventually the better model might be:

[
b_{j,L1}
========

b_j+\delta_{j,L1}
]

for sufficiently supported L1-specific residuals.

But that should come **after** proper empirical validation, not before.

## Priority 0: fix the current release gates before changing the golden

This is a branch-specific issue rather than an academic abstraction.

The repository's own v3 acceptance document says:

> **version 3 is not release-accepted yet.**

The three-seed/eight-document holdout currently fails because only one of three seeds passes all gates. The observed problem cells include idiosyncratic coverage hit as low as **84.375%**, response-noise coverage hit of **87.5%**, and warm-start coverage degradation as large as **4.6875 percentage points**.

The separate holdout report confirms that several cold profiles also still use essentially all 80 questions.

I would not weaken the gates.

The next algorithmic work should try to explain **why** those failures happen. My first experiments would separately vary:

1. evidence reliability;
2. `epsilon_knowledge`;
3. difficulty-prior SD;
4. posterior-predictive quantile;
5. warm-prior mixture weight.

Then determine whether failures are caused by coverage-deck optimization or by the underlying knowledge model.

## Priority 0/1: investigate warm-start coverage degradation before celebrating the 85% question reduction

An 83–86% question reduction is excellent.

But the holdout says some of that efficiency currently comes with worse coverage reliability.

This is exactly why your warm-vs-cold **non-inferiority gate** is valuable.

For the reader profile itself, I would eventually replace the hard:

```text
fresh <= 180 days
otherwise stale
```

rule with gradual uncertainty diffusion.

Vocabulary ability changes over time. Modern dynamic-IRT work explicitly models a time-varying latent ability rather than treating θ as permanently fixed. ([Sage Journals][13])

A simple production approximation could be:

[
\theta_{t}
\sim
N(\theta_{t-1}, \sigma^2_{\text{drift}}\Delta t)
]

so the prior becomes progressively wider with elapsed time.

Then old information isn't suddenly valid on day 179 and worthless on day 181.

You don't need a full MCMC dynamic IRT implementation in the app. Convolution of the 121-point grid with a time-dependent Gaussian would be enough.

## Priority 1: validate Apple POS tagging independently

Lemma+POS is theoretically a good unit.

But now POS errors are **measurement errors that affect persistent lexical identity, calibration pooling and duplicate detection**.

The thresholds:

```text
top probability >= 0.65
top - second >= 0.20
```

are engineering choices, not validated probabilities.

Build an offline English/German test corpus from licensed Universal Dependencies data and report:

* POS precision;
* POS recall;
* abstention/unknown rate;
* error by language;
* error by genre;
* noun↔verb and adjective↔participle confusion;
* resulting lexical-item split/merge error.

The `unknown` fallback is a good defensive design. Keep it.

## Priority 1: don't enable domain blending until it predicts residual difficulty

The current developer gating is exactly right.

The scientific question shouldn't be:

> Is this a fiction book?

It should be:

> After accounting for general frequency and other lexical features, does the item's domain-frequency profile explain additional held-out variance in learner difficulty?

Fit something like:

[
b_j
===

\beta_0
+\beta_1F_{\text{general}}
+\beta_2\Delta F_{\text{fiction}}
+\beta_3\Delta F_{\text{news}}
+\beta_4\Delta F_{\text{reference}}
+\ldots
]

with regularization.

Compare on **participant-and-document held-out data**.

If genre adds no meaningful improvement, don't ship the complexity.

If it does, don't necessarily classify each book into one hard category. Use the detector scores as a mixture:

[
P(domain\mid document)
]

and integrate over domain uncertainty.

The 2025 contextual-diversity review is a good warning here: form-access effects are robust, but semantic effects are task-dependent and substantially less simple. ([Springer Link][8])

## Priority 1: add lexical features to the difficulty prior—but hierarchically

Once you have enough human data, I would replace the current handcrafted rank→mean/SD formula with a hierarchical difficulty model.

Potential predictors:

[
b_j =
f(
frequency,
prevalence,
contextual\ diversity,
word\ length,
morphology,
POS,
cognateness_{L1},
domain\ profile,
\ldots)
+
u_j
]

The crucial part is **partial pooling**.

Rarely calibrated words borrow strength from lexical features; well-observed words increasingly rely on their empirical item difficulty.

Ha et al. and Canning et al. strongly support going beyond raw frequency. ([Sage Journals][4]) The 2025 contextual-diversity review suggests that diversity may add useful form-familiarity information, although semantic effects need careful operationalization. ([Springer Link][8])

This is a much stronger use of embeddings/corpus features than my original semantic-neighbor idea.

Use features to predict **difficulty**, not another item's knowledge state.

## Priority 1: don't treat 98% as the only sensible target

Your UI correctly says 98% is lexical coverage rather than comprehension. Keep that.

But research doesn't support a universal discontinuous threshold at 98%. Song and Reynolds found coverage to be a continuous predictor and found no sharp threshold; 98% was reasonable for their narrative texts, while expository text without background knowledge could require still higher coverage. ([ScienceDirect][14])

Eventually I would expose something like:

| Goal                    | Target |
| ----------------------- | -----: |
| Light preparation       |    95% |
| Comfortable reading     |    98% |
| Maximum lexical support |    99% |

Keep 98% as the recommended/default value if desired.

Do **not** label those with comprehension percentages.

And I would change the wording from:

> Reach 98% coverage

to something closer to:

> **Build a deck for 98% projected coverage**

because the current calculation treats selected cards as known **after they have been learned**. Creating a card is not the same as having mastered it.

Even better, show both:

```text
Estimated current coverage: 94.1%
Projected after mastering selected words: 98.3%
```

That removes a subtle but important conceptual ambiguity.

## Priority 1: the research profile's self-rated proficiency should stay a research covariate

Current code does this correctly.

A very recent 2026 study found that bilingual people's self-ratings changed after completing a difficult vocabulary test, including ratings in the language that had not itself been tested. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][15])

So do not turn:

```text
selfRatedProficiency = "B2"
```

into a strong production θ prior.

For research purposes, also change the free-form text box to a bounded, pre-assessment enum, e.g.:

```text
A1/A2
B1/B2
C1/C2
Unknown / prefer not to say
```

Otherwise your DIF fitter will see `"B2"`, `"upper-intermediate"`, `"b2"`, `"intermediate +"`, etc. as unrelated groups.

## Priority 1: create a real calibration-study schema distinct from the normal privacy export

Your validation pilot correctly requires a **participant-and-document split**.

But the ordinary privacy export deliberately contains no document identity. That's good for normal use, but it means `fit_vocabulary_calibration.py` cannot perform the document holdout described by the validation plan; it only performs participant-based folds.

These should be two intentionally separate data contracts:

```text
ProductResearchExport
    privacy-minimized
    no document linkage
    useful for exploratory calibration

ConsentedValidationStudyDataset
    opaque study document ID
    criterion label
    randomized audit sampling information
    permitted only under explicit study consent
```

Then your confirmatory validation tooling can enforce:

```text
participant disjointness
AND
document disjointness
```

instead of relying on the protocol document alone.

## Priority 2: add multi-word lexical items before same-POS sense clustering

This is probably the most valuable lexical expansion after lemma+POS.

Consider:

```text
take off
put up with
in spite of
by and large
bear in mind
red tape
```

A reader can know every individual component and still not know the expression.

Read's assessment review specifically points out that modern vocabulary assessment needs to move beyond isolated words to compounds, phrasal verbs, idioms, lexical phrases and other multi-word units. ([Sage Journals][7])

For a reading application, this is potentially more useful than attempting to split every polysemous noun into WordNet-like senses.

I'd therefore sequence the roadmap as:

**lemma+POS → high-value MWUs → only then same-POS sense splitting.**

## Priority 2: if you add sense splitting, make it book-local rather than dictionary-global

Your current reserved `senseKey` architecture is a good foundation.

I would not attempt to map every occurrence to hundreds of dictionary senses.

Ask instead:

> Does this particular book use this lemma+POS in two meaningfully incompatible ways?

For example:

```text
bank/NOUN
 ├── financial institution
 └── river edge
```

But:

```text
run/VERB
```

with five subtly related dictionary senses may not benefit from five cards.

Use context clustering as a **proposal mechanism**, then require offline labeled English/German fixtures and your existing oversplitting gate before enabling it.

## Priority 2: add empirical risk calibration on top of the Bayesian model

This is more experimental, but potentially powerful once you have real learner data.

Your Bayesian fifth-percentile coverage is internally coherent **if the model is reasonably specified**.

Your synthetic failures show precisely the remaining problem:

> posterior certainty can still be wrong when the model family is wrong.

After obtaining an untouched real validation set, you could learn an outer calibration safety margin.

For example, suppose the model says:

```text
5th percentile projected coverage = 98.0%
```

but the validation set shows that this quantity still misses actual 98% coverage too often.

A conformal/risk-control layer could calibrate how much extra deck margin is needed to achieve a desired empirical miss rate on held-out readers. Conformal Risk Control provides a model-agnostic framework for controlling monotone loss functions under exchangeability. ([ICLR Proceedings][16])

I would treat this as **post-pilot research**, not something to add now.

It could eventually give you a useful separation:

```text
Bayesian model
    predicts individual word knowledge

empirical risk calibration
    corrects systematic optimism of the final deck decision
```

## Priority 2: reconsider the cold-assessment stopping target

Gyllstad, McLean and Stewart showed why tiny word samples can be dangerously imprecise for individual vocabulary diagnosis; conventional tests with 5–10 words per frequency band are often inadequate, and they recommend much larger samples for many diagnostic uses. ([Sage Journals][17])

So **do not simply reduce 80 to 20 because 80 feels long**.

But the fact that your current cold synthetic runs so frequently reach 80 means the "adaptive stopping" UX is currently often indistinguishable from a fixed 80-item test.

A better long-term solution is to expose the statistical tradeoff explicitly:

```text
Quick
~25–35 questions
more inferred words / wider uncertainty

Balanced
~40–60

High confidence
up to 80
```

The objective would become:

[
\text{expected reduction in decision loss}
------------------------------------------

\lambda\cdot\text{question cost}
]

rather than trying to find one universal stopping threshold.

# 3. One engineering issue I would fix before performance tuning anything else

The new model is heavier, and your benchmark evidence is correctly refusing to hide that.

Across three retained Release repetitions, 10,000-item cold coverage p95 ranges from 28.78–137.19 ms, while warm coverage reaches **155.69 ms** in one run, above the 150 ms gate. The report explicitly says not all p95 gates pass.

I wouldn't reduce posterior samples blindly.

First profile **how often the full coverage-deck recomputation is happening**.

A promising architecture is:

```text
every answer
    update θ posterior
    update cheap probabilities
    choose next question

only when stopping might be possible
    build 512-sample predictive masks
    compute full conservative deck

final results
    recompute full predictive deck
```

You can also use a cheap 128-sample approximation during question selection/stopping screening and a full 512/1024 computation only for a candidate stop/final result, then verify empirically that this never changes the release decision more than the accepted tolerance.

The current benchmark discipline—keeping all repetitions instead of picking the best one—is exactly right.

# 5. New Pull Request description

# Adaptive pre-reading vocabulary assessment with uncertainty-aware personalization

## Summary

This PR adds an optional **Prepare Vocabulary** workflow for English and German PDF, EPUB, and DOCX documents.

The feature analyzes the vocabulary of the current document, asks a bounded adaptive set of **answer-before-reveal** vocabulary questions, estimates the probability that the reader knows each assessable lexical item, and proposes an editable learning deck before reading.

The core problem is that a book may contain thousands of unique lexical items, while testing every one of them would be impractical.

Prepare Vocabulary therefore treats word knowledge probabilistically:

1. build the lexical population of the current document;
2. assign uncertainty-aware cold-start item-difficulty priors;
3. estimate a posterior distribution over the reader's latent vocabulary ability;
4. treat assessment responses as noisy evidence rather than infallible labels;
5. adaptively select questions according to expected reduction in document-wide decision loss;
6. periodically validate high-confidence predictions;
7. construct an editable unknown-word or lexical-coverage deck;
8. optionally reuse completed assessments as a local per-language reader prior on later books.

The implementation deliberately distinguishes **verified evidence** from **model inference** and does not claim to identify a reader's exact unknown vocabulary.

---

## Motivation

Traditional vocabulary-size tests estimate a large vocabulary population from a much smaller sample of test items.

Prepare Vocabulary applies the same general measurement principle to a book-specific task:

> Estimate which lexical items in this document are likely to be known without asking about every item individually.

This differs from a conventional vocabulary-size CAT.

A conventional CAT primarily tries to estimate one latent ability parameter accurately. Prepare Vocabulary ultimately cares about decisions over the **specific lexical population of the current document**, so adaptive selection is optimized for reduction in document-wide classification or coverage loss.

The feature remains explicitly probabilistic:

* direct responses can contain error;
* general-language frequency is only a cold-start difficulty prior;
* individual vocabulary knowledge contains idiosyncratic exceptions;
* lexical coverage is not reading comprehension;
* synthetic evaluation is not validation on real learners.

---

## User workflow

### 1. Analyze the document

The reader can open **Prepare Vocabulary** manually or accept a non-blocking preparation offer when a supported document becomes readable.

The workflow supports:

* English;
* German;
* PDF;
* EPUB;
* DOCX.

The reader chooses an assessment objective:

* **Estimate all unknown words**
* **Target 98% projected lexical coverage**

The document is then indexed into assessable lexical items.

### 2. Inspect the lexical inventory

For every candidate, the inventory retains:

* lemma;
* part of speech when confidently available;
* stable lexical-item identity;
* observed document forms;
* occurrence counts;
* representative source position;
* general-frequency information;
* item-difficulty prior;
* optional experimental document-domain metadata.

### 3. Run an answer-before-reveal assessment

Questions initially show the lexical item without its book context or definition.

The reader can answer:

* **I know it**
* **Not sure**
* **I don't know**
* **Not a word/name**

`I know it` is not accepted as positive evidence immediately.

The definition is revealed first and the reader verifies whether the intended meaning was actually correct.

An optional **type a meaning before checking** mode allows the reader to commit a meaning before seeing the answer while keeping production scoring self-verified and local.

Unknown and unsure responses are recorded before revealing learning content.

### 4. Review probabilistic results

Results distinguish between evidence and estimates:

* verified known;
* reported unknown;
* not sure;
* estimated known;
* uncertain;
* estimated unknown;
* excluded.

The UI exposes estimated `P(known)` for inferred items rather than silently converting unasked words into confirmed states.

The proposed deck remains editable.

### 5. Create & Review

Selected lexical items are imported through the existing vocabulary-record persistence path.

Already-saved records are protected from duplication.

A successful batch import opens the existing Review Session.

## Lexical identity: lemma + part of speech

Prepare Vocabulary now uses a more conservative lexical unit than broad word-family grouping.

A stable lexical identity contains:

```text
language
lemma
partOfSpeech
optional senseKey
```

The production `senseKey` is currently unset.

Examples that can remain distinct include:

```text
book/NOUN
book/VERB

lead/NOUN
lead/VERB
```

Inflected forms belonging to the same lemma and POS remain grouped.

Part of speech is obtained from Natural Language lexical-class hypotheses. A POS is accepted only when the leading class is sufficiently dominant; ambiguous cases remain `unknown` rather than being aggressively split.

Legacy lemma-only records remain wildcard-compatible so the migration does not create duplicate vocabulary entries.

### Sense splitting remains disabled

The model contains a reserved same-POS sense-disambiguation seam, but production does not currently use it.

Sense splitting is intentionally gated until a labeled English/German fixture demonstrates adequate disambiguation accuracy without harmful oversplitting.

No remote language model is required for lexical identity.

## Difficulty priors

Every assessable lexical item receives an uncertainty-aware difficulty prior:

```text
VocabularyItemDifficultyPrior {
    mean
    standardDeviation
    source
    version
}
```

### English

English cold-start difficulty uses the pinned ECDICT frequency scale.

### German

German cold-start difficulty uses the pinned Leipzig `deu_news_2025_1M` frequency resource.

### Ranked items

Frequency rank determines the cold-start mean difficulty.

Unlike the previous fixed-difficulty implementation, ranked items also receive non-zero prior uncertainty.

### Unranked items

Valid but unranked lexical items are not discarded.

They receive:

```text
mean difficulty = 4
standard deviation = 1.5
```

so the model can represent:

> this appears difficult according to the available corpus, but the item difficulty itself is poorly known.

Nine-point Gaussian quadrature integrates item-difficulty uncertainty into item response probabilities.

## Empirically calibrated item support

The difficulty-provider seam can optionally consume a reviewed empirical calibration pack.

An empirical item can replace its frequency-derived prior only when the bundled calibration entry satisfies production eligibility requirements.

Current production gates include:

* sufficient independent learner count;
* bounded item-difficulty standard error;
* no flagged material DIF;
* explicitly reviewed calibration pack;
* supported model/version.

Offline calibration output is always generated with:

```text
reviewed: false
```

and therefore cannot affect production merely because a fitting script has been run.

No empirical LeafReader learner calibration pack is currently treated as established ground truth.

## Reader model

The assessment uses a one-dimensional Rasch-like latent reader model.

For reader ability `θ` and item difficulty `b`:

```text
P(known | θ, b) = sigmoid(θ - b)
```

Item-difficulty uncertainty is integrated rather than replacing `b` with a single exact value.

The reader posterior is represented over a fixed grid from `-6` to `6`.

A broad generic prior is used for a cold reader unless an eligible local per-language reader profile is available.

---

## Responses are noisy evidence

Direct answers no longer force item probabilities to exactly zero or one.

The model stores evidence categories including:

```text
verifiedKnown
typedVerifiedKnown
verifiedUnknownOrPartial
reportedUnknown
unsure
legacyKnown
legacyUnknown
excluded
```

Each non-excluded category has a versioned cold-start reliability assumption.

Evidence updates the latent reader posterior through a noisy-observation likelihood.

The same evidence is also used when reconstructing an answered item's posterior probability.

This makes the model robust to several realities:

* self-verification mistakes;
* partial knowledge;
* uncertain judgments;
* migrated legacy responses;
* person-item exceptions to a one-dimensional frequency/ability curve.

Evidence reliability values are explicitly treated as engineering cold-start assumptions rather than measured human accuracies.

---

## Validation questions and model mismatch

A single latent vocabulary dimension cannot explain every reader's vocabulary history.

Readers may know unusual specialist words while missing more frequent ones, and direct responses may also contain noise.

The assessment therefore periodically tests predictions from the model's high-confidence tails.

Validation contradictions update a bounded model-error estimate and prevent the assessment from immediately stopping after its confidence has just been challenged.

Warm-start assessments deliberately include early current-document tail checks before they are allowed to terminate quickly.

---

## Initial calibration and adaptive selection

Cold assessments begin with items spanning the available difficulty distribution rather than clustering all initial questions around one arbitrary level.

After initial calibration, normal question selection maximizes expected reduction in the current document's decision loss.

For an unasked lexical item with probability `p`, binary classification uncertainty is:

```text
min(p, 1 - p)
```

The global loss is approximately:

```text
L = Σ weight(item) × min(Pknown(item), 1 - Pknown(item))
```

For each candidate question, the assessor simulates the posterior after both possible latent outcomes and computes the expected remaining loss.

The item with the highest expected loss reduction is preferred.

To keep large inventories responsive, expensive scoring is restricted to a deterministic shortlist containing both:

* probability-boundary candidates;
* high-document-impact uncertain candidates.

---

## Assessment modes

### Estimate all unknown

This mode optimizes lexical-item classification.

Unasked items contribute equally to classification loss.

The proposed learning list includes evidence-backed unknown items plus inferred items falling below the unknown decision boundary.

### Target 98% projected lexical coverage

This mode optimizes occurrence-weighted lexical-token coverage.

An item occurring many times therefore matters more to the final deck than an equally uncertain item appearing once.

The target means:

> projected lexical-token coverage if the selected learning items become known.

It does **not** mean:

* 98% comprehension;
* 98% probability the reader understands the document;
* guaranteed present-tense knowledge immediately after records are created.

---

## Posterior-predictive coverage

Version 3 replaces the earlier lower-θ/normal-approximation coverage bound with a posterior-predictive calculation.

Coverage uses 512 deterministic samples over:

* reader ability;
* item-difficulty uncertainty;
* soft answer evidence;
* latent lexical-item knowledge.

For each predictive world, document-token coverage can be calculated directly.

The displayed conservative coverage estimate is the fifth percentile of the sampled coverage distribution.

Selected learning cards are treated as known in the projected post-learning distribution.

### Deck construction

For small fixtures, candidate subsets can be exhaustively enumerated and compared with an exact minimum-cardinality oracle.

For large inventories, the production path:

1. identifies the worst predictive coverage tail;
2. greedily selects high-impact cards;
3. removes redundant cards after reaching the target.

The large-inventory result is therefore a conservative, locally reduced deck rather than a claim of globally minimal cardinality.

## Adaptive stopping

The maximum scored-question count remains 80.

### Cold assessments

Without an eligible reader profile, normal assessments require at least 20 scored responses when the inventory is large enough.

### Warm assessments

An eligible local reader prior can reduce the minimum to 8 questions, subject to current-document validation requirements.

### Stop reasons

Explicit stop states include:

* exhausted candidates;
* low expected value;
* stable target coverage;
* question limit.

The final result exposes the stop reason and residual uncertainty.

## Cross-book reader adaptation

Completed assessments can update a separate local per-language reader posterior.

The profile intentionally excludes:

* passive reading exposure;
* dictionary lookups;
* document text;
* document identity;
* inferred "likely known" states.

Only completed assessment evidence contributes.

A warm profile currently requires:

* at least two completed sessions;
* at least 40 verified responses;
* compatible algorithm version;
* a profile no more than 180 days old.

When eligible, the stored posterior is smoothed and mixed with the generic prior:

```text
90% smoothed local profile
10% generic prior
```

New documents still require direct tail validations before warm-start early stopping is trusted.

Reader profiles stay on-device and can be reset from Vocabulary settings.

## Real-learner validation protocol

Synthetic evaluation cannot establish that LeafReader probabilities are calibrated for real readers.

This PR therefore includes a dedicated empirical validation pilot specification.

The proposed independent criterion is collected **before LeafReader reveals the definition or document context**:

1. show lemma + POS;
2. ask for a typed book-relevant meaning or translation;
3. score responses against a frozen bilingual rubric;
4. use two blinded human raters;
5. adjudicate disagreements;
6. report inter-rater agreement.

Production self-verification is intentionally not reused as its own ground truth.

The proposed pilot also requires:

* multiple English/German learner strata;
* multiple genres/formats;
* participant-and-document holdouts;
* a frozen confirmatory test set;
* complete small-document lexical audits;
* probability/frequency/POS/occurrence-stratified large-document audits;
* stored inclusion probabilities;
* delayed stability/retention retests.

Primary planned outcomes include:

* Brier score;
* log loss;
* calibration plots/ECE;
* calibration slope/intercept;
* theta-interval coverage;
* deck precision;
* occurrence-weighted unknown-word recall;
* realized post-learning token coverage;
* nominal-98% miss rate;
* question/time burden;
* warm-vs-cold non-inferiority;
* DIF and subgroup calibration.

Comprehension remains a separate exploratory outcome.

## Interpretation and known limitations

Prepare Vocabulary remains an uncertainty-aware **cold-start estimator**, not a completed psychometrically calibrated vocabulary test.

Current limitations include:

* evidence-reliability coefficients are engineering assumptions rather than empirically estimated reader accuracies;
* general corpus frequency remains the main production difficulty source;
* empirical LeafReader item calibration is not yet available as validated production evidence;
* one-dimensional ability cannot represent every specialist or idiosyncratic vocabulary profile;
* part-of-speech tagging remains dependent on Natural Language model quality;
* same-POS polysemy remains unsplit;
* multi-word lexical units are not yet independently assessed;
* domain-frequency blending remains experimental;
* self-verification is less independent than a blinded meaning-recall criterion;
* a selected learning card is treated as known for projected post-learning coverage even though creating a card does not itself demonstrate mastery;
* 98% lexical coverage is not a universal comprehension threshold;
* current v3 synthetic release-holdout and warm performance gates are not yet fully passing.

The implementation intentionally keeps these uncertainties visible rather than converting them into stronger product claims.

---

## Follow-up validation path

The intended next stage is empirical rather than architectural:

1. complete the independent real-learner pilot;
2. estimate evidence reliability from criterion-scored responses;
3. improve offline DIF analysis;
4. calibrate item difficulty with participant/document holdouts;
5. evaluate whether lexical features or domain-frequency residuals improve held-out prediction;
6. validate warm-start non-inferiority;
7. enable additional model complexity only when it produces material held-out gains without subgroup regressions.

Domain blending, same-POS sense splitting and 2PL therefore remain deliberately gated rather than being activated simply because implementation seams exist.

The central recommendation after this second review is therefore fairly different from my first one: **the model now has enough sophistication. The highest-value work is to make its calibration data statistically cleaner and its validation loop internally consistent.** I would prioritize the observation-model/calibration-fitter mismatch, proper DIF, calibration-question design, and the failing v3 holdout before introducing another production modeling dimension.
